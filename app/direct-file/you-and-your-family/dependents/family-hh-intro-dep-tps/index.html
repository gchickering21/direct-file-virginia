What’s a Tax Identification Number (TIN)?

A Tax Identification Number (TIN) is:

  • A Social Security number (SSN)
  • An Individual Taxpayer Identification Number (ITIN), which is a type of tax ID number issued by the IRS
  • An Adoption Taxpayer Identification Number (ATIN), which is a temporary number while you’re waiting to finalize domestic adoption

You’ll need to know what kind of TIN they have so that we can determine if they qualify for tax benefits. You’ll only need to enter their TIN once you decide to claim them as a dependent.

If they don’t have a TIN, you won’t be able to claim them as a dependent.

Learn how to get a TIN.

What is an Identity Protection (IP) PIN?

The IRS IP PIN is a 6-digit number assigned to eligible taxpayers to help prevent the misuse of their Social Security number or Individual Taxpayer Identification Number on fraudulent federal income tax returns. A new IP PIN will be generated each year.

If the IRS assigned an IP PIN to someone on your tax return, you must use it to confirm their identity on any return filed during the current calendar year. This includes current year returns as well as any delinquent tax returns.

If someone doesn’t already have an IP PIN, they may get an IP PIN as a proactive step to protect themselves from tax-related identity theft.

What if they changed their legal name last tax year?

They will need to report their legal name change to the Social Security Administration (SSA), if they haven’t done so already. SSA will update their record and mail them a free replacement card.

The name and Social Security number used on a federal tax return must match what’s on their Social Security card. If they don’t match, there could be delays in processing your tax return and issuing any refunds.

What if their name changed since getting their ITIN?

If they have an Individual Taxpayer Identification Number (ITIN), they’ll need to report their legal name change to the IRS at the address listed under "Where do I file my Form W-7/W-7(SP)?" in the Form W-7 Instructions.

They’ll need to submit documentation to support their legal name change, such as a copy of their marriage license, divorce certificate, or other official court-related documents validating the name change.

Who are descendants and direct ancestors?

Descendants are the direct line of relationships starting with someone’s children and continuing down through their grandchildren and great-grandchildren. This also includes stepchildren and children through adoption.

For example:

  • Your child’s descendants include your grandchildren, great-grandchildren, and great-great-grandchildren.
  • Your sibling’s descendants include their children, grandchildren, great-grandchildren, and great-great-grandchildren.

Direct ancestors are also related through parent-child relationships, but they start with someone’s parents and continue up through their grandparents, great-grandparents, and great-great-grandparents.

What if someone in my family is legally separated, got divorced, or died?

Sometimes, family relationships by marriage can be considered for tax purposes, even if the marriage ended in legal separation, divorce, or death.

For example:

  • If your uncle divorces his wife, you can consider his ex-wife as your aunt.
  • If you are divorced, you can consider your ex-spouse’s parents your in-laws.

What’s considered full time?

A full-time student is someone who meets their school’s full-time attendance requirements for 5 months in a calendar year. The 5 months don’t have to be consecutive. Also, the 5 months don’t have to be 5 full months. For example, a student starting a semester mid-January and graduating mid-May attends the school for 5 months.

The student must go to a school with:

  • A regular teaching staff
  • A course of study
  • An enrolled student body

A school includes:

  • High schools
  • Colleges
  • Universities
  • Technical, trade, and mechanical schools
  • Full-time, on-farm training courses offered through a school or a state, county, or local government agency

A school doesn’t include:

  • On-the-job training courses
  • Correspondence schools
  • Organizations offering only internet courses

What is permanent disability?

The IRS uses the term "permanently and totally disabled" to describe permanent disability for tax purposes.

Someone is considered permanently and totally disabled if both of these are true:

  • They can’t engage in any substantial employment because of a physical or mental condition.
  • A doctor finds that the condition has lasted or can be expected to last continuously for at least a year or can lead to death.

Learn more about substantial employment

The CDCC is a tax benefit that helps taxpayers who paid for the care of a family or household member while working or looking for work.

As you complete your federal tax return, we’ll ask questions to check if the CDCC applies to you.

You won’t be asked to send any records with your federal tax return, but the IRS may ask for records later.

  • Records of an accident

What is Form 8332?

A custodial parent can use Form 8332 (Release/Revocation of Release of Claim to Exemption for Child by Custodial Parent) or other written declaration, indicating they won’t claim the child as a dependent on their federal tax return. This allows the child’s other biological or adoptive parent to claim the child on their federal tax return. They can also use Form 8332 to revoke that decision for the current tax year. Learn more about Form 8332.

Identifying who is the custodial parent

When a child’s biological or adoptive parents are divorced, separated, or lived apart during the last 6 months of the year, the custodial parent is the parent with whom the child lived longer.

If both parents lived with the child for an equal amount of time, the custodial parent is the person who has the higher Adjusted Gross Income (AGI). Learn more about the rule for children of divorced or separated parents (or parents who live apart).

Declaration and revocation forms

A custodial parent can choose to sign a written declaration to not claim the child using the IRS’s Form 8332 or a similar statement, including a divorce decree or agreement. Acceptable forms can vary by year. This form or a similar statement is required to allow two taxpayers to get tax benefits for the same child, which is otherwise not allowed. Learn more about the declaration process.

A parent may also use Form 8332 to revoke a declaration. For the tax year, the parent must have given written notice revoking a declaration to the other parent in . Learn more about the revocation process.

Learn more about custody laws

We’re asking these questions about majority status and legal custody to check if the child was subject to custody for the length of time needed to meet the requirements of the IRS’s rule for children of divorced or separated parents (or parents who live apart).

Once someone reaches the age of majority for their state, they are no longer subject to custody laws, but some states do permit custody to go past the age of majority in certain circumstances like disability. Follow your state’s rules when answering this question.

Gaining majority

A person’s age of majority is when they become an adult legally. The age of majority is 18 years old in most U.S. states, territories, and Washington D.C., with these exceptions:

  • The age of majority is 19 years old in Alabama and Nebraska.
  • The age of majority is 21 years old in Mississippi and Puerto Rico.

Minors can also be granted majority status in ways other than reaching their state’s age of majority. This includes minors who were emancipated, married, or joined the armed forces.

What counts as a night lived with a parent?

A child is considered as living with a parent for a night if the child sleeps either:

  • At that parent’s home, even if the parent isn’t present
  • In that parent’s presence outside of the parent’s home (for example, when the parent and the child are on vacation together)

What’s AGI?

Your Adjusted Gross Income (AGI) is your total income minus certain expenses and payments.

Your AGI is important. It helps to determine if you’re eligible for certain tax credits and deductions and how much you can claim.

In some cases, your AGI is also used to decide which taxpayer can claim a qualifying child for benefits as part of the IRS’s tiebreaker rules.

Direct File will calculate your AGI after you provide more information about your income and deductions, but for now, you can estimate your AGI by:

  • Estimating your total income
  • Subtracting the educator expenses adjustment, if you think you might qualify (maximum $300 per taxpayer)
  • Subtracting the student loan interest deduction, if you think you might qualify (maximum $2,500 total)

Subtracting the educator expenses adjustment and student loan interest deduction from your total income will give you an estimate of your AGI, which you can compare with the other biological or adoptive parent’s AGI to determine whose is higher.

Who is the custodial parent?

The custodial parent is the parent with whom the child lived for the greater number of nights during the year. The other parent is the noncustodial parent.

If the parents divorced or separated during the year and the child lived with both parents before the separation, the custodial parent is the one with whom the child lived for the greater number of nights during the rest of the year.

A child is considered as living with a parent for a night if the child sleeps either:

  • At that parent’s home, even if the parent isn’t present
  • In that parent’s presence outside of the parent’s home (for example, when the parent and the child are on vacation together)

If the child lived with each parent for an equal number of nights during the year, the custodial parent is the parent with the higher Adjusted Gross Income (AGI).

Emancipated

If a child is emancipated under state law, the child is treated as not living with either parent.

Absences

If a child wasn’t with either parent on a particular night (for example, the child was staying at a friend’s house), the child is treated as living with the parent with whom the child normally would have lived for that night, except for the absence. But if it can’t be determined with which parent the child normally would have lived or if the child would not have lived with either parent that night, the child is treated as not living with either parent that night.

Parent works at night

If, due to a parent’s nighttime work schedule, a child lives for a greater number of days, but not nights, with the parent who works at night, that parent is treated as the custodial parent. On a school day, the child is treated as living at the primary residence registered with the school.

Learn more in IRS’s Publication 501.

Why do you need to know if their custodial parent is using them for tax benefits?

You said one of ’s biological or adoptive parents signed a written declaration stating they would not claim as a dependent in , and that they meet the requirements of the rule for children of divorced or separated parents (or parents who live apart).

This means they’re allowed to split the tax benefits they can get with as their qualifying person, and you may be allowed to use as your qualifying person for certain benefits, too.

What are the costs of keeping up a home?

When calculating the cost of keeping up the home, include costs such as:

  • Rent
  • Mortgage interest
  • Real estate taxes
  • Insurance on the home
  • Repairs and maintenance
  • Utilities
  • Food eaten in the home

Don’t include:

  • Clothing
  • Education
  • Medical treatment
  • Vacations
  • Life insurance
  • Transportation

If the total amount you paid is more than the amount others paid, then you paid more than half of the cost of keeping up the home.

Where are these work restrictions listed on a Social Security card?

The Social Security Administration issues 3 types of Social Security cards. All cards include the person’s name and Social Security number.

For people who aren’t U.S. citizens or lawful permanent residents, there may be a restriction listed on the front of their Social Security card.

There are 2 types of restrictions:

  • Valid for work only with DHS authorization
  • Not valid for employment

For more details, visit the Social Security Administration’s website.

What if you contributed over 10% of their support and had a multiple support agreement?

Sometimes no one provides more than half of a person’s support. If two or more people together paid for more than half of the person’s living expenses, then they can decide who will claim that person as long as they each contributed more than 10% of that individual’s support.

This requires filing Form 2120 (Multiple Support Declaration), which Direct File doesn’t support yet.

If you want to file Form 2120, consider other ways to file your federal taxes.

What if they earned income from sheltered employment?

Sheltered employment is when a person with a physical or mental disability works for minimal pay under a special program.

If they were permanently and totally disabled and earned income from sheltered employment, answer Yes.

If people with physical or mental disabilities work for minimal pay, it must be done at a qualified location.

They have to be working at that location mainly because they’re getting medical care there too. The income they make must come only from activities at that location that are related to their medical care.

Qualified locations include:

  • Work centers certified by the Department of Labor (formerly referred to as “sheltered workshops”)
  • Hospitals and similar institutions
  • Homebound programs
  • Department of Veterans Affairs (VA) sponsored homes

What does it mean to be a U.S. resident?

For tax purposes, you’re a U.S. resident if either of these apply to you:

  • You meet the green card test or the substantial presence test for the calendar year (January 1 – December 31)
  • You have an active election with the IRS to treat a nonresident spouse as a resident for tax purposes

Learn about tax residency status.

What’s a U.S. national?

When filing a federal tax return, a U.S. national is someone who’s not a U.S. citizen but who owes allegiance to the United States.

U.S. nationals include American Samoans and Northern Mariana Islanders who choose to become U.S. nationals instead of U.S. citizens.

How do I know who can claim someone as a qualifying child?

There are several factors that determine if someone can claim an individual as a qualifying child. The IRS calls these "tests", and you can think of them as different sets of requirements. They include:

  • Relationship
  • Age
  • Residency
  • Support
  • Joint return

Not everyone is required to file a tax return each year, but it may benefit them to file one anyway.

For U.S. citizens and residents, generally, the question of whether or not they must file a tax return depends on their gross income, filing status, and age.

Talk to the person you think could claim you to find out if they’ve got a filing requirement and whether or not they’re filing a return that includes you as a dependent.

How do dependents affect my taxes?

Dependents can bring down the amount someone pays in taxes. They can also increase any refund a taxpayer might get.

Dependents may also qualify you to use certain filing statuses or receive certain tax credits.

As a parent, can I let another taxpayer claim my child?

Only one person can claim as a dependent on their tax return. If you choose to, you can let another qualified taxpayer claim them as a dependent instead of you.

In order for you to let another taxpayer claim this child as a dependent, the child must meet requirements to be that person’s qualifying child and that person’s Adjusted Gross Income (AGI) has to be

  • Highest among all the people who can claim
  • Higher than yours

What is an Identity Protection (IP) PIN?

The IRS IP PIN is a 6-digit number assigned to eligible individuals to help prevent the misuse of their Social Security number or Individual Taxpayer Identification Number on fraudulent federal income tax returns. A new IP PIN will be generated each year.

If the IRS assigned you (or anyone on your tax return) an IP PIN, you must use it to confirm your identity on any return filed during the current calendar year. This includes current year returns as well as any delinquent tax returns.

If you don’t already have an IP PIN, you may get an IP PIN as a proactive step to protect yourself from tax-related identity theft.

What if I file my federal tax return without the IRS-assigned Identity Protection (IP) PIN?

If the IRS assigned you (or anyone on your federal tax return) an IP PIN, you must use it to confirm your or their identities on your federal tax return.

If you file your federal tax return electronically without the IRS-assigned IP PIN, your federal tax return will get rejected.

Alternative federal tax filing option
If you don’t have the IP PIN, you have another filing option: you can file a paper federal tax return. Note, the IRS will need to review your paper federal tax return to check your identity. This review process could delay any refund.

If you need to file a paper federal tax return, you can still use Direct File to fill out your tax return. You would need to then print out your forms to mail to the IRS. Learn about the steps to file by paper.
